Finding Your Style — Why Your Early Work Should Look Copied

Photography · 13 August 2026 · 5 min read

Every photographer wants a signature look. Almost nobody is told the uncomfortable truth about how you actually get one — you copy, deliberately, until something of your own falls out.

The Anxiety of the Blank Style

New photographers agonise over originality — desperate to have a recognisable style, ashamed that their work looks like someone else's. This anxiety is misplaced and actively slows them down. Nobody arrives with a style. Style is not chosen from a blank page; it is the residue left behind after years of imitation, experiment, and slowly noticing what you keep reaching for. Your early work is supposed to look borrowed.

Copying as Curriculum

The fastest way to develop is to find work you love and deliberately reverse-engineer it — recreate the lighting, match the grade, study why it works. This is not plagiarism; it is practice, the same way musicians learn by playing others' songs before writing their own. Each photographer you imitate deposits a technique in your toolkit. Copy ten photographers you admire and you do not become the eleventh clone — you become a unique blend that exists nowhere else, because no one else admired that exact ten.

Style Is What You Can't Help Doing

Real style is rarely a decision. It is the sum of your unconscious preferences: the way you keep underexposing for mood, always framing tighter than instructed, gravitating to warm tones, choosing stillness over action. You will not notice these tendencies while making individual photos. They become visible only across a large body of work — which is why volume matters more than agonising. Shoot enough, and your style reveals itself to you, often before you would have chosen it.

How to Actually Speed It Up

Shoot constantly, imitate openly, and periodically review your own archive for patterns — the shots you are proudest of usually share hidden DNA. Then lean into that DNA deliberately. Consistency in editing helps enormously: a repeatable grade across your work makes a style legible to viewers even before it is fully formed in you. The photographer with a clear, consistent look built from honest imitation beats the one still waiting to feel original before they begin.

Originality is not where you start; it is what is left over after you have honestly copied everyone you admire. Give yourself permission to look derivative, and your own voice arrives faster than if you had waited for it.
