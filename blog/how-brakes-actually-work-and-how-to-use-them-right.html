How Brakes Actually Work — And How to Use Them Right

Motorcycle Education · 21 June 2026 · 7 min read

Most riders use about 30% of their brakes capability. Understanding weight transfer unlocks the other 70%.

The 30% Most Riders Use

Ask a rider to brake as hard as possible in a straight line on dry pavement and most will use roughly a third of what the bike and tyres are actually capable of. Not because they lack strength in their hand — because they have never been taught what happens to the bike's weight during hard braking, and that gap in understanding makes them stop short of the limit out of instinct, not skill.

The other 70% of braking capability is not locked behind better brake pads or a more expensive bike. It is unlocked by understanding weight transfer — a concept that takes ten minutes to explain and changes how every braking event feels from that point forward.

Weight Transfer — The Core Idea

When a motorcycle brakes, its weight shifts forward, onto the front tyre. The front tyre's contact patch grows and its available grip increases. The rear tyre's contact patch shrinks and its available grip decreases. This is why a motorcycle can use far more front brake than rear brake during hard braking — the front tyre, loaded with weight, can handle it. The rear, unloaded, cannot.

This is also why grabbing the front brake gently and progressively works better than grabbing it hard immediately. A gentle initial squeeze gives the forks time to compress and the weight time to transfer forward, which increases the front tyre's available grip just as you are asking more of it. Grab hard immediately, before the weight has transferred, and you ask for grip the tyre does not yet have — this is how front-end lockups happen on otherwise dry, grippy pavement.

The Progressive Squeeze Technique

Practice this in an empty car park: start your braking with light pressure, then progressively increase pressure over roughly one second as the bike's nose dips and the forks compress. By the time you reach maximum brake pressure, the weight transfer is complete and the front tyre can handle it. This single technique, practiced deliberately, is the fastest way to extend your usable braking power without any change to the bike.

Why Trail Braking Exists

Trail braking means continuing to apply a small amount of front brake while leaned into a corner, rather than releasing the brakes entirely before tipping in. It exists because braking while leaned keeps the front tyre loaded and therefore gripping — an unloaded front tyre mid-corner has less available grip for cornering forces, not more. A small amount of trailing brake pressure can actually increase available grip at the front during the early phase of a corner.

This is an advanced technique and should be built gradually, with very light pressure, on a track or empty road before being relied upon anywhere with real consequences. The principle, however, is worth understanding even before you practice the technique itself: braking and grip are not opposites. Done correctly, braking can increase available grip rather than reduce it.

ABS — What It Actually Changes

ABS prevents the wheel from locking once you have already asked for more braking force than the tyre can handle. It does not increase the tyre's maximum grip — it only intervenes after that limit is reached, modulating pressure faster than a human hand can. This means ABS is a safety net for mistakes, not a replacement for understanding weight transfer. Riders who understand braking properly rarely trigger ABS in normal riding, because they are working within the tyre's limit rather than exceeding it and being saved by the system.

Most riders brake the way they were taught to brake when they were nervous and inexperienced, and never revisit it once they have more skill. The brake lever has more in it than almost anyone uses. The only thing standing between you and it is understanding what the front tyre is doing underneath you.

Practicing This Safely

Find an empty, flat car park. Start at low speed — 20 to 30 km/h. Practice the progressive squeeze: light, then building, over about a second, both brakes together with more weight on the front lever. Increase speed gradually as the technique becomes natural. Never practice this in traffic or on the road for the first time — build it somewhere a mistake costs nothing.
