Staying Informed Without Drowning — A Creator's Media Diet

Tech Industry · 16 August 2026 · 5 min read

The afternoon edition closes on the skill that made it possible: consuming tutorials, tech, and world news deliberately, without letting the feed consume you back.

Thirty Afternoons, One Underlying Skill

This second daily edition covered tutorials, tech industry shifts, general knowledge, and geopolitics — and writing it every afternoon depended on one skill worth ending on: staying genuinely informed without being drowned by the firehose of modern media. That skill is now as fundamental as any craft technique.

The Problem With the Firehose

Endless feeds are engineered to maximise your attention, not your understanding. They reward outrage and novelty over accuracy and depth, leave you feeling informed while actually just agitated, and consume the hours that could have gone to making things. Passive, algorithm-fed consumption is to knowledge what junk food is to nutrition — abundant, engineered to be craved, and quietly harmful in quantity.

Building a Deliberate Media Diet

Choose sources, do not just accept feeds. A few trusted, substantive sources beat an infinite scroll of whatever the algorithm serves. Prefer depth over frequency. One thoughtful article beats fifty headlines; understanding beats awareness. Timebox it. Decide when and how long you consume news, rather than letting it leak into every idle moment. Ask what you can act on. The geopolitics posts mattered because they connected to real decisions; information you cannot use is often just anxiety with a headline. Consume to understand and act, not to feel busy.

The Close

Ninety posts across two editions — morning craft and business, afternoon knowledge and news. The thread through all of it: understand deeply, build deliberately, depend on fewer things you cannot control, and stay genuinely informed without letting the feed run you. Thank you for reading. The work continues, on a sustainable schedule, from here.

Being informed is not consuming the most; it is understanding what matters and can be acted on. Choose your sources, prefer depth, timebox the feed — and let information serve your work instead of replacing it.
