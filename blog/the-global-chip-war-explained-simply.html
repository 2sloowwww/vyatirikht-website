The Global Chip War, Explained Simply

Business & Geopolitics · 21 July 2026 · 6 min read

Almost every device you own depends on a chip made by a handful of companies in a handful of places. That concentration has become one of the defining geopolitical stories of the decade.

Why Tiny Chips Move Whole Economies

Semiconductors — the chips inside phones, cameras, cars, and every computer — are the most complex objects humanity mass-produces. The catch: the most advanced ones are made by remarkably few companies, using machines built by essentially one company in the Netherlands, concentrated heavily in Taiwan and South Korea. That concentration turns a component into a geopolitical pressure point.

The Core Tensions

Three threads make this a "war." First, Taiwan: the world's most advanced chips are made there, making the island's stability a global economic concern, not just a regional one. Second, export controls: leading economies restrict which chips and chip-making tools can be sold to rivals, using access to advanced semiconductors as leverage. Third, reshoring: countries are pouring subsidies into building domestic chip capacity, because depending on a distant, contested supply chain for something this essential now looks like a strategic risk.

Why It Reaches India — and You

India is investing heavily to build its own semiconductor manufacturing, aware that a digital economy resting on imported chips is exposed. For a creator, this is not abstract: chip supply affects the price and availability of cameras, phones, storage, and computers — the tools of the trade. The gear inflation covered in the morning edition traces partly back to exactly this story.

The smallest object in your devices sits at the centre of the biggest economic contest of the era. Understanding the chip war is understanding why your tools cost what they cost — and why nations now treat sand-and-silicon as strategy.
