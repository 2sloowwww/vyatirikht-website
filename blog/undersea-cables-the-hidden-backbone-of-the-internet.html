Undersea Cables — The Hidden Backbone of the Internet

Business & Geopolitics · 25 July 2026 · 5 min read

The internet feels wireless and weightless. In reality, almost all of it travels through a few hundred physical cables lying on the ocean floor — and that fact is quietly geopolitical.

The Internet Is Mostly Underwater

When you video-call someone overseas or load a website hosted abroad, your data almost certainly travels through a submarine cable — a bundle of fibre-optic strands, some no thicker than a garden hose, running for thousands of kilometres along the seabed. Satellites carry a tiny fraction; the overwhelming majority of intercontinental internet is these physical cables. The "cloud" has a very wet, very tangible floor.

Why This Is a Strategic Concern

A few hundred cables carry the world's data, landing at a small number of coastal stations. That concentration creates vulnerability: cables can be damaged by anchors, earthquakes, or deliberate sabotage, and a cut in the wrong place can slow or sever a region's connectivity. Who builds, owns, and can reach these cables has become a genuine security question, with nations and tech giants alike investing to control the routes their economies depend on.

Why It Matters to India

India's booming digital economy rides on cables landing on its coasts, and new ones are laid regularly to add capacity and resilience. For anyone whose work depends on the internet — which now means nearly everyone — it is worth knowing that your connection is not magic in the air but glass on the seabed, and that its security is a real-world, physical concern.

The internet is not a cloud. It is a few hundred fragile cables on the ocean floor — and understanding that reveals why connectivity is now treated as critical, contested infrastructure.
