White Balance Explained — Why Your Photos Look Orange

Photography · 23 July 2026 · 5 min read

Light has colour, your brain hides it, and your camera does not. Understanding white balance is the difference between photos that feel right and photos you can't explain the wrongness of.

The Lie Your Brain Tells You

Walk from afternoon sun into a tungsten-lit room and everything still looks normal — white paper looks white in both. That is your brain performing continuous colour correction. Cameras refuse to lie. They record the light as it actually is: streetlights orange, shade blue, mixed indoor lighting a genuinely strange soup. When a photo "looks off" and you cannot say why, white balance is the answer more often than any other setting.

Colour Temperature in One Paragraph

Light colour is measured in Kelvin. Candlelight sits around 1,800K (deep orange), household bulbs near 2,700–3,200K (warm), midday sun around 5,500K (neutral), overcast sky 6,500K, and open shade up past 7,500K (blue). White balance is just telling the camera which of these to treat as neutral. Set it warmer than the scene and everything goes orange; cooler, and everything goes blue.

Auto Is Fine — Until It Isn't

Modern auto white balance handles single-source scenes well. It falls apart in exactly the situations that matter commercially: mixed light (window + tungsten + LED panel in one room), scenes dominated by one colour (a jewellery shot on red silk will fool it), and any series that must match across frames — because auto re-decides every shot, your product catalogue drifts subtly from image to image. For client work I set Kelvin manually and keep it locked for the whole set.

The RAW Escape Hatch — and Its Limit

Shooting RAW means white balance is fully adjustable later, which removes panic but not discipline. Two limits remain: mixed lighting cannot be fixed with one global slider — the window side and the bulb side of a face need different corrections — and a wildly wrong preview misleads every exposure and styling decision you make on set. Get close in camera; perfect in post.

Warmth is a choice, not an accident. The difference between a photo that glows and a photo that looks like a mistake is whether the photographer chose the colour of the light on purpose.
