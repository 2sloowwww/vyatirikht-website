How to Price Your Photography — The Framework I Use

Photography · 30 June 2026 · 7 min read

Pricing is where most photographers undersell themselves. Not because their work is not worth more.

Why Photographers Underprice Themselves

Almost every photographer I know — myself included, early on — has gone through a period of charging less than the work was worth, not because the work lacked value but because pricing was based on anxiety about losing the client rather than on an actual calculation of cost, time, and value delivered. Underpricing is rarely a reflection of skill. It is almost always a reflection of an unclear or absent pricing framework.

The fix is not simply 'charge more,' which is advice without a method behind it. The fix is building an actual framework that accounts for every cost involved, so that pricing becomes a calculation rather than a guess made under pressure during a client conversation.

Start With Your True Hourly Cost

Most photographers price based on the hours spent shooting, completely ignoring editing time, which is frequently equal to or greater than shooting time. A two-hour shoot that requires six hours of editing is an eight-hour job, not a two-hour job, and pricing should reflect the full eight hours, not the two hours that happened to involve a camera.

Calculate your target annual income, divide by the realistic number of billable hours you can sustain in a year — accounting for admin, marketing, and slow periods, not a theoretical 40-hour week of pure client work — and that gives you your real minimum hourly rate. Most photographers, doing this calculation honestly for the first time, discover their current pricing does not come close to covering it.

Pricing by Package, Not Just by Hour

Pure hourly pricing creates an uncomfortable dynamic where efficiency is penalised — a faster editor earns less for the same quality output. Package pricing, based on deliverables rather than hours, rewards skill and efficiency and gives clients clearer expectations of exactly what they receive. A 'product photography package: 20 edited images, two-day turnaround' is easier for a client to evaluate and easier for you to price consistently than an open-ended hourly quote.

Build two or three clear packages at different price points for your core service types. This also speeds up your own sales process significantly — instead of custom-quoting every enquiry from scratch, you are presenting an existing, well-reasoned structure.

Accounting for Usage and Licensing

A significant amount of value in commercial photography is not the time spent shooting — it is the usage rights granted for the images afterward. A small business using images only on their own Instagram account represents far less commercial value than a brand using the same images in a national advertising campaign. Most photographers charge the same regardless of usage, leaving substantial value uncaptured in larger commercial deals.

Build usage and licensing scope into your quotes for any client beyond small local businesses. This single addition to a pricing framework often represents the largest jump in revenue per shoot for photographers who move into more commercial work.

The Margin Check Before Quoting

Before sending any quote, run a quick margin check: subtract travel costs, equipment depreciation and maintenance, assistant or second-shooter costs if applicable, and your own editing time at your calculated hourly rate, from the proposed price. What remains is your actual margin. If that margin is too thin to be worth the work and the risk involved, the price needs to go up regardless of what you think the client is willing to pay — a job priced below sustainable margin is not worth taking even if it fills a quiet week.

Clients rarely push back on price as often as photographers fear they will. They push back on unclear pricing and unclear value. A confidently presented, well-reasoned price is accepted far more often than a nervously discounted one — clients can sense uncertainty in a quote, and uncertainty invites negotiation.

Building Your Own Pricing Sheet

Calculate your true hourly cost honestly, including editing time and annual overhead. Build two or three clear packages per core service. Add a usage and licensing tier for commercial clients. Run the margin check before every quote, every time, even for clients you like and want to work with — liking a client is not a substitute for a sustainable price.
