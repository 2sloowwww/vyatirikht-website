Global Supply Chains — Why "Made Everywhere" Became Fragile

Business & Geopolitics · 2 August 2026 · 5 min read

For decades the world optimised supply chains for cheapness. A run of shocks revealed the hidden cost of that efficiency — and reshaped how goods reach you.

The Machine That Makes Modern Life

Almost nothing you own is made in one place. A single product may contain parts from a dozen countries, assembled in another, shipped through several more. For decades this global supply chain was optimised relentlessly for one thing: lowest cost. It worked so well it became invisible — until it broke.

What "Just-in-Time" Really Traded Away

To save money, companies adopted "just-in-time" production — holding minimal inventory, with parts arriving exactly when needed. Efficient, until a disruption anywhere ripples everywhere: a pandemic, a blocked shipping canal, a factory shutdown, a regional conflict. With no buffer, one broken link stalls the whole chain. The world learned that maximum efficiency and resilience are opposites, and it had bought a lot of the first at the cost of the second.

The Shift Now Underway

In response, the logic is changing from "cheapest" to "cheap enough, but resilient." Companies are diversifying suppliers, moving production closer to home or to friendlier countries, and holding more inventory as insurance. India features heavily in this rethink as businesses seek alternatives to concentrating everything in one country. It is the same resilience lesson the morning edition applies to a single small business, playing out at the scale of the entire global economy.

The world optimised supply chains for cheapness and discovered the bill comes due as fragility. The correction — resilience over pure efficiency — is reshaping where everything you buy is made.
