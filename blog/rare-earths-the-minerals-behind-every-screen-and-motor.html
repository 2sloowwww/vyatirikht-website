Rare Earths — The Minerals Behind Every Screen and Motor

Business & Geopolitics · 29 July 2026 · 6 min read

Your phone, camera, EV motor, and headphones all depend on a group of obscure metals whose supply is concentrated enough to be a genuine geopolitical lever.

The Metals You Have Never Heard Of, In Everything You Own

"Rare earth elements" are a group of seventeen metals with names like neodymium and dysprosium. Despite the name they are not especially rare in the ground — but they are difficult, dirty, and expensive to extract and refine, and one country dominates that refining capacity. That concentration, not scarcity, is what makes them strategic.

Why They Matter

These metals make the powerful compact magnets inside your camera's autofocus motor, your headphones, hard drives, EV motors, and wind turbines. There is no easy substitute for the performance they deliver. Which means whoever controls their processing holds quiet leverage over the supply chains of consumer electronics, clean energy, and defence alike — and that leverage has been used, and threatened, in trade disputes.

The Global Scramble

In response, countries including India are racing to develop their own mining and refining, secure supply agreements, and invest in recycling to recover these metals from old devices. It is the same pattern as the chip war: a concentrated, essential input becomes a strategic priority the moment nations realise how dependent they are. For a creator, it is another thread connecting the price and availability of your gear to distant geopolitics.

The tiny magnets that focus your lens and drive electric motors depend on metals refined mostly in one place. Rare earths prove again that in modern tech, control of an obscure input is control of the whole chain.
