How We See Colour — Light, Eyes, and Why Screens Lie

General Knowledge · 24 July 2026 · 5 min read

Colour feels like a property of objects. It is actually an event happening inside your head — and understanding that changes how you think about photography, screens, and print.

Colour Is Not in the Object

A red apple is not red. It reflects wavelengths of light that your eyes and brain interpret as red. Colour is a collaboration between light, a surface, and a nervous system — which is why it can be manipulated, mismatched, and misremembered so easily.

Three Cones and a Trick

Your eye has three types of colour receptors, roughly tuned to red, green, and blue light. Every colour you have ever seen is your brain's reading of how strongly those three are triggered. Screens exploit this shamelessly: a display has no yellow pixels, yet you see yellow — because it lights red and green pixels together and your three-receptor system cannot tell the difference. All of digital colour is this trick, repeated millions of times.

Why Colour Never Quite Matches

The same photo looks different on your phone, your laptop, and in print because each device produces colour differently — screens emit light (RGB), print absorbs it (CMYK), and no two panels are calibrated identically. This is why professionals calibrate monitors and why a client's "the colours look off" is usually about mismatched devices, not a mistake. Colour is consistent only when you control the whole chain.

Colour is not a fact about the world; it is your brain's interpretation of light. Once you accept that, mismatched screens and tricky white balance stop being mysteries and start being physics you can manage.
